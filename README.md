The Journey of Numbers
====

Edition: $\displaystyle \phi = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2} = 0.618$

This repository contains the text of the popular math book, _The Journey of Numbers_. The book provides a historical introduction to numbers. As a central notion in mathematics, numbers connect various areas and  theories. This book includes 33 interesting stories and biographies of great mathematicians in the history of numbers. The book is available in English ([PDF](https://github.com/user-attachments/files/28531753/amathtour-en.pdf)) and Chinese (中文) ([PDF](https://github.com/user-attachments/files/28538604/amathtour-zh-cn.pdf)).

Forward
--------
I had a chance to sit in on my child's online class during the Covid-19 pandemic in 2022. The entire city was under lockdown, and we had to stay home, working and learning remotely, with no sense of when it would end. It was a hard time for everyone, teachers in particular. They pushed on to deliver courses through the winter semester even after falling ill and feeling unwell. Without face-to-face interaction and timely feedback, teaching was difficult; it was all a matter of persistence.

As a father, I wondered what I could do to help. Human knowledge accumulates generation after generation. What we teach children today is highly condensed. School mathematics, for instance, is presented as a finished body of knowledge, in a perfect state of done. Behind every theorem lie brilliant minds and fascinating stories: Pierre de Fermat's private mathematical inquiries, the contest between Cardano and Tartaglia over cubic equations, the unknown young man who uncovered the geometric meaning of complex numbers... These elegant results emerged from trial, error, frustration, and delight, yet classroom time is too limited to share these tales with children. This is where parents can step in: once the pandemic passed, we could take our children to museums to learn this history, meet these figures, and grasp why these wonderful discoveries came to be. 

Mathematics grew out of the pursuit of truth and beauty. Every claim demands rigorous justification. No one holds absolute authority; great mathematicians including Fermat and Euler made mistakes. Mathematics advances through the continual identification and correction of flaws. Many times in history, mathematicians rebuilt large parts of the subject from entirely new foundational frameworks. Many mathematicians were true scholars. When the nineteen-year-old, little-known Lagrange wrote to Euler about independent discoveries in the calculus of variations, Euler held back his own work so Lagrange could publish first: “In no way do I want to take away from you any of the glory that you deserve.” When Emmy Noether was barred from a faculty position simply because she was a woman, Hilbert defended her, saying: “The university is not a bathhouse.” Mathematics is beautiful in its clarity, elegance, concision, and abstraction. Kronecker argued in his writings that mathematics is closer to an art than a science. Erdős traveled from university to university across the globe, searching for the most beautiful _proofs from THE BOOK._ Euler's identity is widely regarded as the most beautiful formula. The golden ratio has been deliberately used in fine art. Stunning fractal patterns appear everywhere in nature...

This book aims to build a virtual museum tracing the journey of numbers — a journey in pursuit of truth and beauty. It is not a textbook and does not compete with textbooks; nor is it merely a collection of puzzles. I have tried to avoid gimmicks and brain‑teasers. Even so, each chapter ends with exercises, with complete answers provided in the appendix. Working through the problems independently before checking the solutions is strongly encouraged. I wrote this carefully as a popular science work. Its narrative follows roughly the chronological development of numerical concepts throughout history. The book addresses many common "why" questions: Why does multiplying two negative numbers yield a positive? Why is division by zero undefined? Why do the decimal expansions of some fractions repeat infinitely? Why is the decimal expansion of $\pi$ endless? It also answers many "how" questions: How did ancient people approximate $\pi$? How did people compute square roots by hand? How can one construct a regular pentagon using only a straightedge and compass? How did mathematicians discover the radical formula for roots of cubic equations? It also explores historical curiosities: Who first introduced the fraction bar? Who invented the decimal point? Who devised the percent symbol? What is a base-point? Why is the base of the natural logarithm named e? The book also re-examines some widely circulated legends. Pythagoras likely did not discover musical harmonies by listening to blacksmiths at work. The story that Hippasus was drowned at sea for discovering irrational numbers is almost certainly fictional. And the aspect ratio of most books and paper sizes is not the golden ratio...For readers: the first half requires only elementary school mathematics; the second half calls for high-school mathematics. Post-medieval methods for computing $\pi$ touch on basic calculus. Starting from Chapter 4, the book covers elementary number theory — an unavoidable stop on the journey of numbers.

Contents
--------

- Forward
- Chapter 1, **Numeral systems** \
Covers numeral systems in ancient Egypt, Babylonia, Rome, China, and Maya. Shows how language influenced numbers. Introduces the widely used Hindu–Arabic (positional decimal) numeral system and explains why it has advantages for calculation. Briefly introduces the binary numeral system and computers.
- Chapter 2, **Zero** \
Introduces the notion of zero in ancient India and how people rejected, debated, and finally accepted it. Covers confusion and debates about negative numbers and their acceptance. Explains why "two negatives make a positive" and presents how to model negative numbers as pairs of natural numbers.
- Chapter 3, **Fractions** \
Pythagoras music tuning and fractions. Egyptian fractions; the Babylonian base-60 fractional system; arithmetic rules of fractions in ancient China. Indian fractions and the fraction bar symbol. Fractions and decimals, including repeating decimals. Extension of numbers with fractions.
- Chapter 4, **Irrational numbers** \
**(a) All is number.** The Pythagorean school. **(b) Pythagoras's theorem.** Various proofs of the Pythagorean theorem. **Number theory.** Pythagorean triples, perfect numbers, Fermat numbers, and Mersenne numbers. **(c) Irrational numbers.** Irrational numbers from geometric constructions. **(d) Euclidean algorithm.** The Euclidean algorithm and its relation to irrational numbers and continued fractions.
- Chapter 5, **Real numbers** \
**(a) Geometric constructions.** Arithmetic of geometric constructions. Coordinate geometry and geometric construction. Regular polygon construction and its limitations. **(b) Pi.** Pi in ancient times. Geometric approximations. Binomial theorem. The fundamental theorem of calculus. Series, and Leibniz formula. Lambert's proof that Pi is irrational. **Real.** Eudoxus's theory of proportion and Dedekind cuts.
- Chapter 6, **Complex numbers** \
**(a) Cubic equations.** Geometric and algebraic solutions to quadratic and cubic equations; the emergence of imaginary numbers in cubic equations. **(b) Interpretations of complex numbers.** Geometric interpretation and the Argand diagram. Algebraic interpretation as a pair of real numbers by Hamilton. The fundamental theorem of algebra. **(c) e.** History of e and its definition as a limit. Euler's identity and the relation between e and the logarithmic spiral. **(d) Regular Pentagon.** Geometric construction of the regular pentagon with complex numbers.
- Chapter 7, **Algebraic numbers** \
**(a) Fermat's Last Theorem and complex numbers.** Infinite descent and Fermat's proof for n = 4; Euler's proof for n = 3 with interpretations using complex number. **(b) Ideal numbers.** Failure of unique factorization in Lamé's attempt to prove Fermat's Last Theorem. **(c) Gaussian integers.** An example of algebraic integer. **(d) Quadratic numbers.** Quadratic numbers and failure of unique factorization. **(e) Ideals.** Ideals as a way to restore unique factorization. **(f) Infinite sets.** One-to-one correspondences for comparing infinite sets; countable and uncountable infinities; R as an uncountable set.
- Appendices and answers \
**A.** Answers to all exercises; **B.1.** Proof of the commutativity of addition and multiplication of natural numbers; **B.2.** Algorithm to search the "best" Egyptian fraction decomposition. **B.3.** Even perfect numbers; **B.4.** Proof scheme of the Gauss–Wantzel theorem; **B.5.** A combinatorial proof of Fermat's little theorem; **B.6.** Some limits; **B.7.** Tangent function in continued fractions; **B.8.** Formula for the Fibonacci sequence via a generating function. **B.9.** Wilson's theorem. **C.** Greek letters.
