\chapter*{Forward}

\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Forward}

I had a chance to listen to my kid's class during the Covid-19 pandemic in 2022. The entire city was locked down, and we had to stay at home, working remotely and learning remotely without knowing when it would be an end. It was a hard time for all, in particular, for teachers. They tried their best to continue the courses over the winter semester although all were infected and in bad condition. Without face to face interaction, without effective feedback, it's hard; it's all about insisting.

As a father, what can I help? People discovered more and more knowledge age by age. What we teach to our children is highly summarized. For example, the mathematics taught is in a state of done. There were great minds and interesting stories behind each theorem: like Pierre de Fermat's `secrete study', the knightly challenge between Cardano and Tartaglia for the cubic equations, the young man who discovered geometric meaning of complex numbers but forgot to leave his name... Those beautiful result arose from failures, errors, tears, and joys, but we don't have enough time in the class to tell them to our children. This is exactly what our parents may help: when the pandemic is over, visit the museum with our kids to understand the history, the people, and the `why' for those beautiful achievements.

Mathematics is about and arose from pursuing truth and beauty. Everything has to be justified rigorously. There is no authority; great mathematicians, like Fermat and Euler made mistakes. It is through the continuously identifying and correcting defects that mathematics progress. There were many times in history, that people completely rebuilt mathematics on top of a genuine new foundation. Many mathematicians were true scholars. When the young Lagrange, at the age of 19 who was unknown to the public, wrote Euler with overlapping discoveries in the calculus of variations, Euler decided to conceal his result until Lagrange publishing first, ``in no way do I want to take away from you any of the glory that you deserve.'' When Emmy Noether was facing obstacle of being a faculty member only because she was a woman, Hilbert fought for her and say, ``The University is not a bathhouse.'' Mathematics is beautiful in terms of clear, elegant, concise, and abstract. Kronecker argued in his dissertation that mathematics is rather an art than science. Erdős wandered about the world from one university to another, seeking the most beautiful \emph{proofs from THE BOOK}. Euler's identity is considered the most beautiful formula. Golden ratio is consciously applied in beautiful art works. The amazing fractal appears here and there in nature...

The purpose of this book is to show a virtual museum about the journey of numbers, which is a journey of pursuing truth and beauty. This is not a textbook, and doesn't conflict with textbooks; It is by no means a collection of puzzles. I try to avoid any tricks or brain-teasers, nevertheless, every chapter is followed with some exercises and the full answers are given in the appendix. It's helpful to get your hands on before looking up the answer. Indeed, I write it seriously as a popular science book. The content is organized roughly by the chronological order of the notion of numbers in history. The book addresses some common `why' questions, like why do two negatives make a positive? why can't be divided by zero? why do the decimals converted from some fractions repeat endlessly? why the decimal $\pi$ is infinitely long? The book also answers many `how' questions, like how did the ancient people calculate $\pi$? how did them manually extract square root? how to draw a regular pentagon with only ruler and compass? How did mathematicians discover the root of cubic in radicals? Other questions are like who was the first introduced the fraction bar? who invented the decimal point? who invented the percent symbol? what is base point? why the base of natural logarithm is named as $e$? The book questions some popular stories, showing that Pythagoras could not hear harmonic sounds from the blacksmith; it's merely a fiction that Hippassus was drawned at sea for his discovery of irrational number; and the ratio of the width and length of books and papers is not the golden ratio...

For the readers, the first half of the book only requires the school math under K9 grade, and the second half needs the math in high school. The method to calculate $\pi$ after the Mid-Age, touches some basic calculus. The book contains elementary number theory from chapter 4 on. After all, in the journey of numbers, nobody can pass by the number theory.
