I had a chance to sit in on my child's online class during the Covid-19 pandemic in 2022. The entire city was under lockdown, and we had to stay home, working and learning remotely, with no sense of when it would end. It was a hard time for everyone, teachers in particular. They pushed on to deliver courses through the winter semester even after falling ill and feeling unwell. Without face-to-face interaction and timely feedback, teaching was difficult; it was all a matter of persistence.

As a father, I wondered what I could do to help. Human knowledge accumulates generation after generation. What we teach children today is highly condensed. School mathematics, for instance, is presented as a finished body of knowledge, in a perfect state of done. Behind every theorem lie brilliant minds and fascinating stories: Pierre de Fermat's private mathematical inquiries, the contest between Cardano and Tartaglia over cubic equations, the unknown young man who uncovered the geometric meaning of complex numbers... These elegant results emerged from trial, error, frustration, and delight, yet classroom time is too limited to share these tales with children. This is where parents can step in: once the pandemic passed, we could take our children to museums to learn this history, meet these figures, and grasp why these wonderful discoveries came to be.

Mathematics grew out of the pursuit of truth and beauty. Every claim demands rigorous justification. No one holds absolute authority; great mathematicians including Fermat and Euler made mistakes. Mathematics advances through the continual identification and correction of flaws. Many times in history, mathematicians rebuilt large parts of the subject from entirely new foundational frameworks. Many mathematicians were true scholars. When the nineteen-year-old, little-known Lagrange wrote to Euler about independent discoveries in the calculus of variations, Euler held back his own work so Lagrange could publish first: “In no way do I want to take away from you any of the glory that you deserve.” When Emmy Noether was barred from a faculty position simply because she was a woman, Hilbert defended her, saying: “The university is not a bathhouse.” Mathematics is beautiful in its clarity, elegance, concision, and abstraction. Kronecker argued in his writings that mathematics is closer to an art than a science. Erdős traveled from university to university across the globe, searching for the most beautiful \emph{proofs from THE BOOK}. Euler's identity is widely regarded as the most beautiful formula. The golden ratio has been deliberately used in fine art. Stunning fractal patterns appear everywhere in nature...

This book aims to build a virtual museum tracing the journey of numbers — a journey in pursuit of truth and beauty. It is not a textbook and does not compete with textbooks; nor is it merely a collection of puzzles. I have tried to avoid gimmicks and brain‑teasers. Even so, each chapter ends with exercises, with complete answers provided in the appendix. Working through the problems independently before checking the solutions is strongly encouraged. I wrote this carefully as a popular science work. Its narrative follows roughly the chronological development of numerical concepts throughout history. The book addresses many common ``why'' questions: Why does multiplying two negative numbers yield a positive? Why is division by zero undefined? Why do the decimal expansions of some fractions repeat infinitely? Why is the decimal expansion of \(\pi\) endless? It also answers many ``how'' questions: How did ancient people approximate \(\pi\)? How did people compute square roots by hand? How can one construct a regular pentagon using only a straightedge and compass? How did mathematicians discover the radical formula for roots of cubic equations? It also explores historical curiosities: Who first introduced the fraction bar? Who invented the decimal point? Who devised the percent symbol? What is a base-point? Why is the base of the natural logarithm named e? The book also re-examines some widely circulated legends. Pythagoras likely did not discover musical harmonies by listening to blacksmiths at work. The story that Hippasus was drowned at sea for discovering irrational numbers is almost certainly fictional. And the aspect ratio of most books and paper sizes is not the golden ratio...For readers: the first half requires only elementary school mathematics; the second half calls for high-school mathematics. Post-medieval methods for computing \(\pi\) touch on basic calculus. Starting from Chapter 4, the book covers elementary number theory — an unavoidable stop on the journey of numbers.
